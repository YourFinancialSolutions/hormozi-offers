## 1. Read the equation correctly

**Book, pp. 74–76.** The visual model is:

`Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)`

Increase the two numerator factors; reduce the two denominator factors. The equation communicates relationships rather than supplying measurable units or a price calculator. The author's limiting picture is an outcome achieved immediately and effortlessly. Do not compute division by zero or claim literal infinite market value.

The author's emphasis evolves from larger promises and more proof toward shorter waits and easier implementation. A headline promise is easy to copy. A delivery system that removes setup, learning, waiting, and coordination can be harder to reproduce. The strongest improvement may therefore be behind the headline.

One component can affect several factors. A guided setup may shorten time, reduce effort and increase confidence. Classify the main mechanism but do not force each component into exactly one box.

**Implementation:** The skill's 1–10 scorecard is a qualitative comparison instrument. In that scorecard high speed/ease scores mean good, whereas raw delay/effort in the book's denominator should decrease. Do not multiply those scores and call the result dollars or a scientific value index.

## 2. Dream outcome and social meaning

**Book, pp. 77–79.** Sell the destination the buyer wants to reach, not merely the vehicle. A gym membership is a means; a personally meaningful change is the destination. Multiple categories may compete to satisfy the same desire, even when the seller considers them different industries.

The book explores appearance, respect, love, power and status as motivations. The minivan-versus-sports-car anecdote shows that status depends on the relevant group: the same object can increase approval in one social setting and reduce it in another. Do not assume expensive possessions are everyone's aspiration or universalize the book's gender examples.

Translate a functional feature through this chain:

`What changes → what it lets the buyer do/feel → why that matters to them → who, if anyone, notices.`

**Adaptation:** A well-organized expense report may mean less time reconstructing receipts, greater confidence in an accountant meeting, and feeling in control of the business. These are buyer hypotheses to validate, not established motives for all truck drivers.

A shared dream outcome cannot explain a price difference by itself. If two options promise the same destination, examine confidence, speed and effort. This is the point of the book's comparisons among different vehicles for appearance or relaxation.

**Questions:** What would make the purchase worthwhile in the buyer's words? What would they stop worrying about? Is the stated aspiration theirs or the seller's? Can the provider actually support the promised destination?

## 3. Perceived likelihood and proof

**Book, pp. 79–80.** People pay for confidence that a result will happen **for them**. The comparison between a first-time and highly experienced surgeon holds the nominal procedure constant while changing trust in achievement. See E6 in [book-examples.md](book-examples.md).

Relevant proof must cross the buyer's specific doubt. A large testimonial archive may not answer whether this works for a beginner, a small company, or a business with messy historical data. A specific demonstration may be more useful than an impressive but unrelated result.

**Implementation: proof ladder, not a book taxonomy:**

1. Explain the mechanism in terms the buyer can inspect.
2. Demonstrate the process or output on a relevant sample.
3. Show comparable customer evidence with scope and context.
4. Explain qualifying conditions and exclusions.
5. Use a financially supportable guarantee for the remaining risk.

Choose the evidence available rather than fabricating a step. If there are no customer outcomes yet, show a prototype or bounded pilot and label it accurately. A guarantee transfers consequences; it does not prove the underlying success rate.

**Questions:** What makes them believe their case is different? Which part of delivery is observable? What evidence would change their mind? Is the promise more certain than the data justify?

## 4. Time delay and early experience

**Book, pp. 80–82.** Distinguish the long-term result people buy from the short-term experiences that help them continue. Early tangible or emotional wins reinforce the decision while the larger result is still developing. The author describes early campaign activation and a first sale as stepping stones toward a larger gym-business outcome; these are his examples, not a guaranteed seven-day sales standard.

An early win should be meaningful in the path to success. Access credentials, a welcome video, or a dashboard can reduce uncertainty, but they are not automatically customer value. Ask what burden actually disappears or what usable output exists.

**Apply:** Map purchase time, first useful output, first evidence of progress, and final expected result. Identify waits caused by the business, customer, third parties, or physical realities. Shorten what can be shortened. For the rest, make dependencies and progress visible.

**Book, p. 82:** The “fast beats free” idea explains willingness to pay to avoid delay even when a free option exists. It is a positioning hypothesis, not a law. Speed matters only when the buyer values it enough and the result remains reliable.

**Adaptation:** A free blank spreadsheet and a guided import that produces a usable report may satisfy similar needs but impose different setup work. A paid app's speed claim should be demonstrated on real input, including imperfect data.

**Questions:** What can a buyer use on day one? Which wait makes them regret buying? Can the first milestone be delivered without implying that the entire outcome happens immediately?

## 5. Effort, sacrifice, and hidden work

**Book, pp. 82–86.** Price is only one cost. Learning, scheduling, changing habits, embarrassment, giving up enjoyable activities and coordination can all reduce value. The book contrasts self-implementation with outcomes obtained through less personal effort. These are perception examples, not clinical advice or proof that different treatments produce equivalent outcomes.

DFY can increase perceived value twice: less work for the buyer and greater trust that an expert will execute. But outsourcing does not eliminate approvals, data collection or continuing responsibilities. Moving invisible work onto the customer while advertising “effortless” breaks the promise.

**Implementation:** For each journey step, specify who does it, approximate burden, required decisions, inputs and failure recovery. Look for document chasing, repeated entry, unfamiliar terminology, and unnecessary handoffs. Decide what to eliminate, automate, do together, or leave with clear guidance.

The solution need not be “more education.” If a customer wants a completed result, another training module may make the offer feel heavier. A prefilled template with a short review can be more valuable than a large course.

## 6. Logical and psychological solutions

**Book, pp. 76–77.** The transit-display example explains how visible arrival information reduces the discomfort of waiting without making a train faster. Elevator mirrors illustrate changing the experience of a wait rather than only engineering faster machinery. These are author examples inspired by Rory Sutherland, not independently checked studies.

The useful distinction is:

| Friction | Operational improvement | Experience/communication improvement |
|---|---|---|
| A process takes too long | Remove unnecessary steps | Show meaningful progress and next event |
| A result feels uncertain | Improve reliability and qualification | Demonstrate the mechanism and comparable evidence |
| Setup feels difficult | Import data and reduce decisions | Clarify ownership and show a first-step example |

Both columns can be valid. Transparency does not substitute for fixing an unreliable service, and reassuring language must match reality. A psychological solution changes the customer's genuine experience rather than hiding relevant facts.

## 7. Turn diagnosis into a design decision

**Implementation:** For each important improvement, record `buyer doubt → evidence → delivery change → explanation → tradeoff → observable measure`. Compare expected impact against cost and confidence; do not optimize a lever the buyer barely cares about.

If the outcome is compelling but purchase feels risky, work on proof/guarantee. If confidence is high but the buyer is too busy, redesign effort. If the buyer cannot wait, offer a feasible earlier milestone or faster service tier. If the underlying outcome is weak, another convenience bonus is unlikely to solve the problem.

Return an explanation of why the recommended component changes perceived and actual value. Then use the problem/solution process in [knowledge-construction.md](knowledge-construction.md) to build it.
