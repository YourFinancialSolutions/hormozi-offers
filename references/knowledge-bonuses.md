## 1. A bonus is also a presentation choice

**Book, pp. 146–149, 153.** A bundle may contain valuable pieces that the buyer overlooks when it is described as one generic service. Pulling out and naming a distinct useful piece makes its value visible. The book often presents previously assembled deliverables as bonuses; it does not require inventing unrelated extra products after the package is finished.

Establishing the core price and then revealing relevant bonuses can widen the perceived value-price gap without reducing the price. The source strongly prefers this over negotiating the main price downward. The key is that each bonus makes a real obstacle easier to solve or a next step easier to take.

**Important distinction:** Presentation status is not fulfillment necessity. A component can be highlighted separately while remaining included for everyone entitled to the promise. If success depends on it, do not genuinely remove it after a deadline while keeping the same promised result. The skill's core/support/bonus/tier classification is an operational tool, not the author's formal taxonomy.

The book advocates bonus value exceeding perceived core value. Explain that persuasion rationale when asked, but do not convert it into a rule to inflate every bonus total or make the stack confusing. The customer's relevant value matters more than the number of items.

## 2. Build the value story

**Book, pp. 148–149.** A strong bonus presentation covers more than a name and dollar amount:

- Link it to a specific obstacle or anticipated next problem.
- State what the buyer receives in ordinary language.
- Explain its origin: a real repeated problem, development effort, or discovery.
- Show how it improves speed, ease, confidence or the outcome.
- Supply relevant evidence or a demonstration.
- Help the buyer picture using it and experiencing the benefit.
- Explain a defensible value basis.

**Adaptation:** “Receipt Recovery Checklist” becomes meaningful when the seller explains that it helps locate missing documents in email and vendor portals before the accountant review. A claim that it was developed from hundreds of client cases requires actual cases. Without that evidence, show how the checklist works rather than inventing a founder story.

**Implementation:** Keep separate fields for monetary anchor, evidence, time saved and seller cost. A $50 production cost says little about customer value. An estimated avoided mistake is not a verified saving for every buyer. If the bonus is never sold separately, do not claim a fictitious normal selling price.

Avoid double counting: the same onboarding call used to explain three tools is still one call. A partner discount and the full retail value of that same purchase cannot both be counted as independent savings.

## 3. Select assets by effort removed

**Book, pp. 148, 152–153.** Tools and checklists are often stronger than additional training because they require less time and effort to use. Reusable templates, scripts, calculators and concise maps can contain substantial prior work while keeping customer effort low.

The qualifier is whether the buyer can use the tool correctly. A template that requires expert interpretation may need a short orientation. More recordings are not inherently more value; a relevant two-minute demonstration may beat a large archive.

**Implementation ranking:** First identify the obstacle's importance. Then compare how directly each asset solves it, how easy it is to use, how much proof exists, and what delivery/support it costs. Monetary value alone is not a useful ranking.

**Questions:** What will the buyer do with it? When? What does it replace? What mistake does it prevent? Does it create new work? Would its absence affect the main promise or only convenience?

## 4. Present in a conversation or to a group

**Book, pp. 147–148.** In one-to-one selling, the author suggests asking for the core purchase first. An accepting customer can receive a genuine surprise bonus that reinforces the decision. A hesitant customer can receive the bonus matched to the stated obstacle before another invitation to buy.

This is a presentation sequence, not a reason to withhold material terms or manufacture obligations. If the prospect refuses because the product is unsuitable, an unrelated gift is not a solution. When a material inclusion affects the buying decision, explain it before commitment.

The book explicitly says a complete group-selling process is beyond its scope. The skill's public-presentation sequence—core offer and price, then relevant bonuses, proof, summary and CTA—is an implementation framework inspired by the chapter, not a recovered full webinar script.

**Adaptation script structure:** “You mentioned [obstacle]. This included [asset] helps by [specific mechanism]. Here is [demonstration/evidence], and here is what you still need to do. Does that address this concern?” Continue based on the answer, not a quota of closing attempts.

Bonuses can have their own scarcity or urgency when access is actually limited or a genuine inclusion ends. Exclusive availability is different from a countdown; use the correct description.

## 5. Partner economics and the asset bank

**Book, pp. 150–153.** Adjacent providers may offer valuable products, services or discounts in exchange for exposure to suitable customers. A negotiated benefit can improve the main offer at low direct cost. The book also discusses commissions and group discounts, creating a separate potential revenue source.

Do not treat contingent commissions as guaranteed profit. Model redemption, eligibility, exclusions, availability, support and the partner's ability to honor the deal. Keep ordinary customer benefit and seller compensation transparent.

The source includes healthcare-related referral examples; these are illustrations, not evidence that such arrangements are lawful in a user's jurisdiction. For a real regulated offer, verify the applicable constraints before recommending payments or incentives.

**Implementation partner brief:** customer need; proposed benefit; genuine price/savings basis; eligibility; fulfillment owner; expiration; data-sharing arrangement; seller compensation; failure remedy. Prepare the brief without contacting the partner unless authorized.

The asset bank compounds over time. Record reusable tools, recordings and negotiated benefits by customer obstacle and evidence, not just by file name. Review relevance and partner validity before reuse. Existing access in a vault is not evidence that a resource fits the current buyer.

## 6. Choose core, support, bonus, or tier

**Implementation:**

| Finding | Decision |
|---|---|
| The primary promise fails without it | Keep it reliably included; it may be highlighted, but not made unavailable as an optional extra |
| It creates a fast start or reduces a specific adoption barrier | Include as support; highlight if distinct and valuable |
| It resolves a next problem and can stand apart | Consider a bonus with clear use and terms |
| It requires expensive personal work for only some buyers | Consider a priced tier, capped access or separate service |
| It is cheap but irrelevant | Remove |
| It duplicates a benefit or asks the buyer to consume more than needed | Combine, simplify or remove |
