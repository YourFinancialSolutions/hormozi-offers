## 1. Naming makes relevance visible

**Book, pp. 171–173.** A name helps the right person recognize a desirable experience. The float-session versus stress-release-program example holds the underlying service broadly constant while changing the invitation. A functional service name may be accurate yet fail to express why the buyer should care.

The name is the wrapper. It can clarify a strong offer and renew attention, but it does not create capabilities or change terms by itself. Keep the operational specification separate so that creative experiments do not accidentally change fulfillment.

The book describes increased familiarity in local markets and faster fatigue because of a smaller reachable audience. It explicitly says reaching someone once does not prove fatigue. Do not assume one impression equals attention or that a current platform has the media cost reported in this older PDF.

## 2. MAGIC component reasoning

**Book, figure p. 173 and explanation pp. 173–177.**

| Component | What it contributes | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| M: Magnet / magnetic reason | Attention and a reason for this invitation | Why this offer, promotion or opportunity now? |
| A: Avatar | Recognition and selection | Who should know immediately that this is for them? |
| G: Goal | A desirable purpose | What do they want to achieve or experience? |
| I: Interval | Time expectation | What duration or milestone can actually be supported? |
| C: Container | A recognizable structured vehicle | Is it a workshop, system, assessment, sprint, program or another real format? |

The author suggests using roughly three to five components, changing their order when it reads better. Neither all five letters nor a fixed word order is mandatory. An explanatory subtitle can carry useful detail when the name becomes cumbersome.

**Magnet:** The source includes seasonal reasons, openings, giveaways and discounts. A reason need not be dramatic, but it must be real. “New” requires something actually new. The chapter references later money-model material for monetizing free/discounted offers; do not invent those details as part of this volume.

**Avatar:** Specify enough to improve recognition and fit. A neighborhood can be stronger than a large city for a local service, but excessive specificity can exclude viable buyers. Qualification must agree with the named avatar.

**Goal:** It may be an outcome, feeling, event or experience. Connect it to what the buyer values, not an internal task code. Avoid a sensational result the business cannot deliver.

**Interval:** Distinguish program length, time to first useful output, and deadline for a final result. “A two-week workshop” and “double revenue in two weeks” are different commitments. Removing a dollar amount does not make an outcome-by-date promise automatically claim-free.

**Container:** Choose a word that represents the service. A standalone consultation is not a full implementation system merely because “system” sounds valuable. Define any branded phrase in plain language.

## 3. Examples, localization, and claims

**Book, pp. 175–177.** Rhyme and alliteration can aid recall but should not be forced. The author supplies examples across wellness, medical services and coaching, and applies naming to subcomponents/bonuses too. These are creative examples rather than current compliant ad templates.

**Implementation:** In Russian, preserve the function of MAGIC rather than English syntax or rhymes. A natural Russian phrase plus a clarifying subtitle may work better than a literal translation of an English container word. Do not preserve an alliteration at the cost of meaning.

**Adaptation:** “Порядок в расходах тракиста: практикум на 14 дней.” Avatar = truck driver, goal = organized expenses, interval = program duration, container = practical workshop. Clarify that it is a guided practice format, not an existing feature of TruckBooks or a guaranteed financial outcome. Use a different container if the deliverable is only software access.

For real campaign copy, check claim support, eligibility, actual discount basis and platform/jurisdiction requirements where relevant. The PDF alone does not verify current advertising rules. Keep examples about health/finance as illustrative until the real offer's claims are assessed.

The source recommends testing two or three promising names and retaining a winner as a control. The skill's name scorecard is a pretest heuristic, not evidence that a name will convert. Compare qualified response and eventual buying quality, not just clicks.

## 4. Diagnose fatigue before rebuilding

**Book, pp. 177–179.** Refresh lighter elements before redesigning a proven operational model: creative, copy, headline/wrapper, seasonal context, duration, promotional enhancer, and finally monetization/pricing structure. The printed list mixes some examples among the steps; the sequence here groups them by what actually changes.

Changing six weeks to 42 days can be a naming change; changing six weeks of service to four weeks changes delivery. Do not treat both as costless wrapper edits. A new bonus may also have operational cost even when the core product stays unchanged.

**Implementation diagnosis:**

| Evidence | Likely area to inspect |
|---|---|
| Same qualified buyers, same delivery results, declining ad engagement | Creative, message, audience exposure |
| Strong engagement but poorer-fit inquiries | Avatar and promise in the wrapper |
| Same inquiry quality, fewer purchases | Price, confidence, terms, sales execution, alternatives |
| Good sales but more complaints/refunds | Fit, promise, onboarding and delivery |
| All customer pathways declining | Broader market and product relevance; do not assume creative fatigue |

Keep a control offer and change one major factor where practical. The purpose is to retain useful learning, not to prohibit all simultaneous operational improvements. If multiple changes are necessary, record them so results are not attributed to one variable without evidence.

## 5. Goodwill and execution: what the book actually includes

**Book, pp. 87–88:** “Free Goodwill” is chiefly a request for an honest book review after the reader has received value, framed around helping other entrepreneurs. It is not a detailed independent lead-generation system. An applicable lesson is to make a clear, easy, voluntary ask after delivering value. Do not substitute paid positive reviews or compulsory endorsements.

**Book, pp. 184–189:** The final section describes the author's first substantial personal cash reserve, recaps the offer methodology, encourages continued practice and points toward subsequent learning. It is not a step-by-step guaranteed path to $100,000, a 30-day experiment protocol, or a validated offer scorecard.

**Implementation:** The interview rounds, 100-point audit, assumption ledger, validation gates and stop criteria in this skill are additions that operationalize the concepts. Label them accordingly. They help users act but should not be cited as author-prescribed chapter content.

A viable offer is a foundation for acquisition, not evidence that customers will arrive without distribution. Hand off to `$hormozi-leads` when customer acquisition is requested, carrying the actual offer, avatar, proof, price, capacity and defined conversion event. Do not import later-book frameworks into this book's source map.

## 6. Learning and implementation modes

**Explain from the book:** Locate the topic in [book-map.md](book-map.md), read the relevant knowledge section and example, explain the principle and reasoning, include PDF pages, then separate interpretation/limits. Give an exercise if it helps the question. Do not start a full business interview for a conceptual question.

**Apply to the business:** Read the matching procedure and knowledge section, use known facts, ask focused questions only for consequential gaps, and produce a decision with its rationale and next evidence. Mark hypothetical numbers and claims. Do not flood a practical answer with a chapter summary.

**Guided full build:** Complete the commercial stages in order while loading each stage's resources when needed. Preserve a brief working state: buyer, destination, current design, evidence, unresolved facts, decisions and next stage. Show an intermediate map before jumping to sales copy. Resume from that state in later turns.

**Explain a choice:** Give the relevant book principle, its source, how the observed business facts affect it, the selected option, and why an alternative loses. This is how a knowledge library improves decisions rather than merely lengthening outputs.
