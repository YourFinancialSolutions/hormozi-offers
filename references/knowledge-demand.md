## 1. Why manage availability after building value?

**Book, pp. 121–129.** The fundraiser narrative shows that value perception depends on context: rarity, limited access, competition, status and the charitable setting influence willingness to pay. The author explicitly acknowledges several forces beyond the offer enhancers. Do not attribute the entire reported fundraising result to scarcity alone.

The “dance of desire” discussion distinguishes maximizing today's unit sales from earning more per unit and sustaining future interest. Different buyers have different willingness to pay. A smaller premium service can outperform a larger cheap group, but only when enough suitable buyers value the access. See E13 in [book-examples.md](book-examples.md).

The author's suggestion to leave demand unsatisfied is strategic positioning, not a universal economic law. With no supply there is no revenue; with no useful demand, a cap cannot create a desirable product. Keeping a waitlist can also lose customers. Compare profit, customer experience, cash needs, and the opportunity cost of turning buyers away.

The book's delayed-ask idea argues that building value and anticipation can support a larger ask. It does not prescribe indefinitely withholding the price or waiting when qualified customers are ready to buy.

## 2. Three scarcity families and service implementations

**Book, pp. 134–137.** The broad families are limited seats/slots, limited bonuses, and something that will not be offered again. Physical products can use real limited editions or production batches. Services can use distinct capacity rules:

| Service cap | What is limited | Difference that matters |
|---|---|---|
| Total business/service-tier cap | Active clients receiving a defined service | A vacancy depends on someone leaving or capacity increasing |
| Growth-rate cap | New customers onboarded per period | The business may serve many active clients while accepting few new ones |
| Cohort cap | Places in a scheduled group | Both group size and start cadence matter |

**Book nuance:** The author recommends consistently selling out smaller releases and communicating an actual sellout because it affects future expectations. Batch size should reflect credible demand; “sold out” must describe a real result. Monthly releases and 10–20% capacity increases are examples from his experience, not default schedules.

**Implementation:** Specify the denominator whenever saying “almost full.” Is it the founder's calendar, an onboarding team's week, or a product batch? A cap may be a genuine business choice rather than a physical law, but enforce it and describe it accurately.

For a digital product with effectively unlimited copies, do not claim physical scarcity. A genuinely capped support tier, event seat, personal review, or partner allocation can be limited independently. If the seller intentionally closes digital enrollment, describe enrollment availability rather than running out of downloads.

## 3. Expertise, limited access, and re-entry

**Book, pp. 131–133, 137–138.** Specialized expertise can be scarce because few people can solve a particular high-value problem. Buyers may pay for avoided mistakes and faster progress, not the expert's hours. This combines market specificity, proof, time and supply.

High-ticket personal access should use a medium the provider can sustain. An expert who likes scheduled video calls but dislikes continuous messages should not sell unlimited chat just because it sounds valuable. The delivery promise and the actual calendar must match.

The source also describes “leave and cannot return” for small groups. Preserve this as a source tactic, not a default retention rule. A real admission policy must be clearly disclosed and honored; simply threatening exclusion can damage trust. An ordinary capacity-based waitlist is often sufficient. Avoid punishing customers for using promised cancellation or refund rights.

**Questions:** Is personal access solving a high-value problem? Why does this buyer need this person? What is capped? What happens when a customer cancels? Can the business fulfill the policy without ad hoc exceptions?

## 4. Four urgency mechanisms

**Book, pp. 140–144.** Scarcity is about quantity; urgency is about time. A cohort can generate urgency even with unlimited places because waiting means missing the next start.

| Book mechanism | Reason to act sooner | Design detail |
|---|---|---|
| Rolling cohort | Next start date is approaching | A real kickoff and an enrollment cutoff; identify the next opportunity |
| Rolling seasonal promotion | A named campaign has a defined ending | Campaign dates and customer-relevant seasonal context |
| Pricing or bonus deadline | Particular terms are ending | Identify exactly which price, inclusion or service level changes |
| Exploding opportunity | External opportunity loses value with delay | Evidence of actual change outside the marketer's invented timer |

For year-round services, the offer's promotional terms can expire while the business continues operating. The book's roofing example makes this distinction: it would be misleading to imply no future service when only a promotion is ending.

Exploding-opportunity examples in the book include arbitrage and early-platform advantages. They are not instructions to promise financial returns. When using such a claim in a real business, verify the changing facts; do not turn ordinary uncertainty into a fabricated emergency.

## 5. Late buyers and recurring promotions

**Book, pp. 141–143.** For someone arriving after a cohort begins, the book offers two routes: a feasible catch-up onboarding or entry to the next cohort with useful preparation time and potentially different payment timing. Neither requires claiming a cancellation occurred when none did.

**Implementation:** If catch-up is possible, explain the missed work, support required and any additional cost before selling. If not, give the next true start. A deadline can still be honest when there is a predefined late-entry policy; an endlessly waived deadline is not useful information.

Seasonal wrappers can recur around the same core service. Say precisely what ended and what the next campaign offers. If the terms are identical, avoid implying that delaying permanently loses benefits that remain available. The source's February 30 example is a typo; validate real dates.

The source suggests notifying existing prospects before a genuine price increase. This is a campaign-design option; using the skill does not authorize contacting anyone. Draft the message and list requirements unless sending is explicitly requested.

## 6. Choose and measure a real limit

**Implementation:** Start from operations and calendar, not from a persuasive sentence:

1. Find the actual constraint: workload, event date, bonus inventory, production batch, partner terms.
2. Choose the simplest accurately described limit.
3. Identify owner and source of the number/date.
4. Define what happens after the limit and when availability returns.
5. Compare conversion, retained customers, remedy cost, utilization and waiting-list loss.

**Adaptation:** An accounting firm may have capacity for three historical cleanups this month but no cap on a downloadable checklist. Limit the cleanup intake if confirmed; do not attach “three copies left” to the checklist. A software product might have unlimited subscriptions but only four guided migration appointments. Keep those offers distinct.

Do not predict the book's reported late-launch sales percentages for a new campaign. A good test measures whether the limit improved sustainable contribution while the business kept its promises.
