## 1. Find the risk before choosing the remedy

**Book, pp. 155–158, 168.** A prospect may value the outcome and still refuse because of uncertainty about what happens if the purchase fails. Risk reversal can make the exchange easier to accept. “Guaranteed” has little substance until the seller specifies what happens when the promised standard is missed.

The basic structure connects a result or service standard, a time/trigger, and a remedy. Identify whether the buyer fears losing the purchase price, wasted effort, external spending, a deadline, or being trapped in a contract. A remedy should address that fear. An impressive-sounding credit is weak if the buyer's concern is losing cash needed elsewhere.

A guarantee is not proof of outcomes and cannot repair an unreliable product. The source warns about both refunds from weak fulfillment and customers who are interested only in the guarantee. Keep qualification tied to fit and success conditions, without treating reasonable questions about risk as evidence of a bad customer.

## 2. Four families

**Book, pp. 156–159, 166–168.**

| Family | Core risk position | Useful setting | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unconditional | Buyer can claim the stated remedy without performance requirements | Low marginal cost, an inspectable product, reversible service | Seller can fulfill correctly and still owe a refund |
| Conditional | Remedy applies when stated buyer actions and result/service conditions are met | Customer implementation matters and evidence is available | More conditions can reduce perceived strength and complicate claims |
| Anti-guarantee | Explicit final-sale/no-voluntary-refund position with a reason | Irreversible customization or information value, where permitted | More buyer risk; needs trust and honest explanation |
| Implied/performance-aligned | Some payment depends on an agreed result | Measurable outcomes, reliable attribution and collection | Seller finances uncertainty and may struggle to collect |

Unconditional means unconditional **with respect to outcome/implementation requirements**, not necessarily infinite duration. State the actual period, purchase scope and claim method.

The source calls performance pricing an implied guarantee in a commercial sense. This is not a legal definition of an implied warranty. The arrangement may still require a setup fee or minimum, which means the buyer is not literally paying nothing unless successful.

## 3. Remedy library and decision criteria

The following variants are paraphrased from PDF pp. 159–168. Fit/cost considerations are implementation guidance. They are a menu to reason from, not scripts to reuse uncritically.

| Variant | What the book describes | Choose when / avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| No-questions refund, pp. 159–160 | Return all or an agreed portion; source also discusses expanded remedies | Best when inspection is valuable and cost exposure manageable. Define amount and period rather than promising “risk free” vaguely |
| Satisfaction refund, pp. 160–161 | Remedy tied to perceived service value rather than a guaranteed final outcome | Useful where service can be judged but outcome also depends on the buyer; distinguish satisfaction from objective performance |
| Outsized refund, pp. 161–162 | More than purchase price if specified implementation fails | Requires high margin, strong process evidence and reserves. Model the adverse tail, not only expected claims |
| Service until outcome, p. 162 | Continue working without extra fees until the target is reached | Aligns with customers wanting success; potentially unlimited labor and capacity liability |
| Modified service extension, p. 163 | Continue for a stated additional period | A finite commitment can be easier to administer; define work included during the extension |
| Credit-based, p. 163 | Return value as credit for another offered service | Often fits an upsell to an existing satisfied customer; poor match when the buyer distrusts all services or needs cash back |
| Personal service, pp. 163–164 | Founder/expert works directly with the customer | Valuable when personal intervention can actually resolve failure; scarce founder time must be reserved and capped if advertised as capped |
| Travel/ancillary costs, p. 164 | Reimburse specified external expenses as well as the product | Can address workshop travel exposure; define eligible costs, evidence and limits in advance |
| Wage/time payment, pp. 164–165 | Compensate time if a session lacks value | Use an agreed rate or simple basis. Do not make invasive paperwork a deterrent to a legitimate claim |
| Release of service, p. 165 | Remove a commitment or cancellation charge | Addresses fear of being trapped; not a special benefit if the customer already has that right |
| Delayed second payment, p. 165 | Do not collect the next payment before a first outcome | Fits a measurable activation step and enough seller cash runway; define what happens if the milestone never arrives |
| First-outcome expense support, p. 165 | Seller covers specified ancillary costs after a missed first-result deadline | Incentivizes activation; open-ended ad spend or travel needs careful exposure modeling |
| All sales final, p. 166 | No voluntary refund, explained by real vulnerability or irreversible delivery | State the actual reason and applicable terms; do not invent exclusivity or override mandatory rights |
| Payment per performance event, p. 167 | Fee per sale, attendance or other agreed event | Requires exact event definition, exclusion rules, attribution and reliable data |
| Revenue/profit share, p. 167 | Share an agreed financial result or increase from baseline | Define revenue vs collected revenue vs gross/net profit, deductions, refunds and baseline |
| Ratchets and triggers, p. 167 | Rate changes at thresholds or a bonus when a milestone occurs | Specify marginal vs retroactive rates and when thresholds are measured |
| Minimum/hybrid/transition, pp. 167–168 | Floor fee or initial fixed period before performance compensation | Funds setup and support but weakens “no performance, no pay”; describe the minimum truthfully |

The source often favors service-based guarantees and performance partnerships, and recommends more specificity for higher-cost/B2B situations. Treat this as a preference to test against the actual service, not a prohibition on all other types.

## 4. Conditions that support success

**Book, pp. 157–158, 162–164.** Conditions can require actions that genuinely help the customer reach the desired result: attending sessions, supplying data, implementing steps or reporting progress. The ideal is that customers satisfy the conditions and get the result, so they do not need the remedy.

**Implementation:** For each condition, explain its causal relevance. Record how the customer learns about it, how completion is evidenced, and what help exists if they get stuck. If a condition has no relationship to success or fair administration, remove it.

Avoid outcomes the customer cannot control as eligibility requirements. “Provide complete bank exports” can be a reasonable input; “the tax authority must approve your claim” is not a customer action. Define paused timelines for missing required inputs rather than claiming an absolute deadline while hiding exceptions.

The source contains aggressive examples, including inventing an anti-guarantee reason and deterring wage claims through paperwork. Preserve the distinction when explaining the book; implement truthful reasons and accessible remedies. Those adaptations are explicit, not falsely attributed to the author.

## 5. Compare sales, cash and profit

**Book example, p. 156:** 100 sales with 5 refunds leaves 95 retained purchases. 130 sales with 13 refunds leaves 117. `117 / 95 = 1.2316`, about 23% more retained purchases. This does not by itself establish 23% more profit.

**Implementation: consistent comparison.** Use equal exposure or a clearly comparable opportunity pool. Define whether sales acquisition cost changes with exposure, leads, purchases or commission payments. Calculate:

`Contribution = sales × price − refunds × refund amount − fulfillment cost for all served customers − additional remedy costs − acquisition cost`

Do not subtract refunds again when starting from revenue already net of refunds. A returned purchase may still have consumed full delivery cost. A retained client receiving an extension may incur remedy cost without a refund.

**Adaptation, low delivery cost:** Equal campaign spend of $10,000, $500 price, $100 delivery cost per purchaser. Old: `50,000 − 2,500 − 10,000 − 10,000 = $27,500`. Proposed: `65,000 − 6,500 − 13,000 − 10,000 = $35,500`. These are modeled contributions before overhead, with assumed conversion and claims, not forecasts.

**Same adaptation, high delivery cost:** If delivery is $430 per purchaser, old contribution is `50,000 − 2,500 − 43,000 − 10,000 = −$5,500`; proposed is `65,000 − 6,500 − 55,900 − 10,000 = −$7,400`. More retained sales can coexist with a worse loss.

For fixed price and acquisition spend, the simplified nonnegative-contribution requirement with full refunds is:

`sales × [price × (1 − refund rate) − direct cost − other expected remedy cost per sale] ≥ acquisition spend`

Separate expected claims from maximum simultaneous exposure. For extensions, model claim rate × extra hours × labor cost and the resulting service capacity. For performance models, map collection lag, disputes and bad debt. Do not call already-collected cash safe profit when future service remains owed.

## 6. Stack guarantees without contradictions

**Book, pp. 158–159.** An initial unconditional inspection period can be combined with a later conditional outcome remedy. Two conditional guarantees can cover sequential milestones. Each should address a different risk or time period rather than restating the same remedy twice.

**Implementation:** Build a timeline and decide whether remedies are alternatives or cumulative. Can the same buyer claim a full refund and continued free service? Do refunds cancel future performance obligations? Who verifies each milestone? Do all sales materials describe the same terms?

Never hide an undisclosed cap in the internal plan. If a finite extension is the affordable option, sell that version and label it as the modified variant rather than calling it “work until success.”

## 7. Build and evaluate a proposal

Deliver a plain-language draft containing eligibility, service/result standard, customer actions, measurement source, clock start, remedy, claim route and timeline. Label proposed terms as proposed until approved by the business. Recommend one primary structure and explain why a tempting alternative was rejected.

For an existing guarantee, trace complaints and claims back to poor fit, unclear terms, missing inputs, unreliable delivery or misleading sales language. Reducing legitimate claims through obstacles is not product improvement.
