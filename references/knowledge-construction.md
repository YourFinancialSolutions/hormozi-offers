## 1. Divergence before convergence

**Book, pp. 89–93.** Convergent thinking finds an answer from fixed constraints, as in the sales-staff arithmetic example. Divergent thinking generates different valid ways to solve a problem with changing or unknown constraints. Offer construction needs both, in order: first explore possibilities, then select a feasible design.

The two-minute brick exercise changes assumed attributes such as size, material and shape to reveal uses beyond an ordinary building brick. Its purpose is to expose constraints people added without noticing. It is not a mandatory ritual before every offer. Use it when the user keeps proposing the same familiar service.

**Apply:** Name the assumed constraint: “It has to be a weekly call,” “The customer must do the setup,” or “We can only sell hours.” Temporarily relax it and generate alternatives. Then restore actual budget, capability and permission constraints before choosing. Imagining a solution is not a commitment to deliver it.

**Counterexample:** Renaming a weekly consultation three times is not three delivery options. An asynchronous review, a preconfigured tool, and a done-for-you implementation are meaningfully different options.

## 2. Build the obstacle inventory

**Book, pp. 95–100.** Begin with the destination the buyer wants. Identify every important thing they must do to reach it, including what happens before and after the main service. For each action, enumerate reasons they might not start, might struggle, or might stop.

The fitness example expands buying, cooking, eating and exercising into many subproblems: confusion, time, money, preferences, family, travel and sustainability. The four value drivers are prompts, not a cap of four obstacles. The book's 32–64-problem discussion illustrates depth; it does not establish a required list length for every offer.

**Implementation: two passes.**

1. Map the chronological journey without evaluating solutions. Capture buyer language and source: interview, support request, observed failure, or assumption.
2. Revisit each action through outcome-worth, belief, effort and time. Add context exceptions such as travel, switching, other decision-makers, and maintenance.

Do not compress all objections into “needs accountability.” Ask what fails: remembering, knowing what to do, doing it at the right time, receiving feedback, or recovering after a missed week. These may require different components.

Repeated themes are useful during discovery. Later group them without losing distinct contexts. One reusable component may solve multiple rows; several components may be needed for one important obstacle.

**Book, pp. 100–102:** Brooke Castillo's handwritten relationship-program example shows the same mapping outside fitness. The images are rough working notes. They illustrate enumeration and reversal, not a validated treatment protocol or evidence that relationships can be guaranteed in 90 days.

## 3. Separate a solution from its vehicle

**Book, pp. 98–100, 107.** A solution statement reverses the obstacle into a requirement: what must become possible for the buyer? The delivery vehicle explains how the business will make it possible.

| Layer | Question | Adaptation: expense tracking |
|---|---|---|
| Obstacle | Why will the buyer struggle? | “I have a pile of receipts and won't enter them after driving.” |
| Solution requirement | What barrier must be removed? | Capture usable expense records without a large manual-entry session |
| Vehicle | What actually provides that help? | Photo capture with review; voice entry; a guided weekly session; a paid cleanup service |
| Promise | What can be stated truthfully? | Defined capture/review workflow with explicit user responsibilities |

Do not jump directly from a generic pain to the product already on sale. The middle step lets the business consider other ways to solve the problem. Also do not mistake a sentence beginning with “how to” for a fulfilled solution; a real delivery mechanism must follow.

## 4. Explore the delivery dimensions

**Book, pp. 107–110.** Generate vehicles across these dimensions:

| Dimension | Variations | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Individual, small group, one-to-many | Personalization, access, marginal labor |
| Customer work | DIY, done-with-you, done-for-you | Effort, control, training needs |
| Live medium | In person, telephone, video, email, text, chat | Convenience, speed, cost and support burden |
| Recorded format | Written, audio, video | Consumption effort, accessibility, reuse |
| Availability | Response time, service hours/days | Certainty and staffing cost |
| Price thought experiment | What could justify 10×? How could success still happen at one-tenth? | Breaks default assumptions about intensity and reusable assets |

The grocery-shopping example includes accompanying a client, making a personalized list, doing the shopping, group instruction, a recorded tour, a calculator, predefined lists, buddy systems and prepared delivery carts. The important lesson is variation in the **how**, not accumulating all possible formats.

**Implementation:** For a top obstacle, generate options from materially different cells. For secondary obstacles, reuse a proven component when it genuinely fits. Do not exhaust a combinatorial grid solely to make the document longer.

Ask about support precisely. “Access to us” could mean one response per business day or unlimited immediate calls. The value and cost are very different. Model response times together with contact volume and staff availability.

## 5. Balance sales and fulfillment

**Book, pp. 105–106.** Taking more work and uncertainty off the buyer can make selling easier while making delivery harder. Taking less responsibility may ease fulfillment while leaving the buyer unconvinced. The book recommends earning demand and revenue through strong initial delivery, then refining the system.

The Gym Launch story moves from on-site execution and travel to teaching owners the system remotely. The promise remained broadly similar while the mechanism changed. The lesson is not “all DFY should become DWY.” It is that field experience revealed what could be transferred and standardized. If customers cannot implement the remote model, the same change could destroy outcomes.

**Implementation:** In an early pilot, deliberately choose the manual work that produces learning about the critical customer obstacle. Cap the pilot at a fulfillable volume. Record what repeats, what varies and which intervention causes progress. Convert repeats into assets only after understanding the process.

Do not interpret “add friction later” as secretly withdrawing something already sold. Change future packaging transparently, improve qualification, or streamline delivery while honoring existing promises.

## 6. Trim without breaking the promise

**Book, pp. 111–113.** Remove high-cost/low-value options first, then low-cost/low-value options. Preserve high-value/low-cost items and evaluate high-value/high-cost items carefully. Value is judged through the buyer's outcome, confidence, time and effort, not the creator's pride in the asset.

When a valuable solution is too expensive, step back from the intensive version. A private shopping trip may become a recorded store tour plus a personalized list and a brief orientation. Keep the causal mechanism that matters; remove costly delivery features the buyer does not need.

**Implementation decision matrix:**

| Buyer value / business cost | Default decision | Check before deciding |
|---|---|---|
| High / low | Keep | Is it actually used and useful for this avatar? |
| Low / high | Remove or redesign | Is it secretly necessary to fulfill the promise? |
| Low / low | Usually remove | Does it add explanation/choice burden despite low production cost? |
| High / high | Redesign, price appropriately, cap access, or create a tier | Can a lower-cost vehicle preserve the result? |

Cost has two distinct parts: up-front creation and marginal delivery. The meal-plan spreadsheet on p. 112 reportedly took about 100 hours to create and about 15 minutes per personalized plan afterward. The principle is reusable intellectual work, not zero maintenance or free customization.

**Adaptation calculation:** Suppose an asset costs $1,500 to create, reduces direct labor by $35 per customer, and adds $5 support per customer. Net saving is $30; simple labor-cost recovery takes 50 uses. That does not prove demand or include all maintenance and financing costs. Compare near-term expected usage before overbuilding.

**Two different trims:** Remove a redundant delivery option without removing coverage of its obstacle. If every viable vehicle for a required obstacle is unaffordable, narrow the promise or change price/segment. Do not keep the claim while dropping its fulfillment.

## 7. Stack by customer problem

**Book, pp. 113–116.** Bundle retained vehicles into understandable solutions. The source sequence links problem, solution-oriented language, a benefit-led name and actual deliverables. The final gym stack covers shopping, cooking, eating, exercising, travel, adherence and social situations rather than merely listing sessions.

This creates coherence: the buyer sees help with the things that would otherwise defeat the result. Several small assets can form one named system. One introductory meeting can explain several systems without becoming three separate meetings or three separate value anchors.

**Implementation:** Maintain two views:

- **Fulfillment view:** deliverables, owner, timing, responsibilities, cost, capacity, dependencies.
- **Presentation view:** buyer problems, named solutions, benefits, proof and selling sequence.

Core/support/bonus/tier labels in this skill are implementation aids. The book sometimes highlights components of an already-included package as bonuses. A marketing label does not change whether the primary result depends on the component. Keep those dependencies explicit and do not withdraw required support to manufacture urgency.

For naming and bonus presentation, use [knowledge-bonuses.md](knowledge-bonuses.md). Do not assume every source value anchor is a verified standalone selling price.

## 8. Completion checks

**Implementation:** Before copywriting, verify:

1. Every material obstacle is addressed, explicitly excluded through qualification, or marked unresolved.
2. Every kept component has a function and a feasible owner/delivery vehicle.
3. Redundant assets are grouped or removed without losing coverage.
4. High-cost access has a price/capacity reason to exist.
5. The package can be explained through the customer's journey.
6. The business can show the promised result or progress, not merely deliver files.

Use the eating-out story E11 when a seller insists the customer must change a fixed life constraint. Use [worked-offers.md](worked-offers.md) for complete original examples with economics and tradeoffs.
