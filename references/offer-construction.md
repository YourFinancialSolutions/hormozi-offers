# Offer Construction: Problems, Solutions, Delivery Vehicles, Trim & Stack

Use this workflow to construct the core offer before adding enhancements.

Book basis: chapters 8–10, PDF pp. 89–116. Read [knowledge-construction.md](knowledge-construction.md) for explanation, source nuances and examples. See [book-map.md](book-map.md) for citation conventions. Core/support/bonus/tier is an implementation classification; retain a separate record of whether each component is necessary for the promise.

## 1. Use divergent thinking

Generate many valid ways to solve each obstacle before evaluating feasibility. Do not let the current service model constrain ideation.

Use these prompts:

- What would solve this if time and cost were unlimited?
- What would solve this for one person, a small group, or many people?
- What would make the buyer say the package is surprisingly complete?
- What would remove the next objection before it appears?
- What would a ten-times-higher price allow?
- How could the result still be delivered at one-tenth the price?

Separate generation from selection. Premature filtering produces ordinary offers.

## 2. Define the dream outcome

Describe the destination, not the vehicle:

- Weak: "monthly bookkeeping."
- Stronger structure: "[buyer] gains [recognizable business outcome] by [relevant point], with [major burden removed]."

Do not finalize language until the claim is supportable.

## 3. Map problems

Map the complete customer journey:

1. Immediately before purchase or switching.
2. Setup and onboarding.
3. First use.
4. Repeated use or implementation.
5. Progress and troubleshooting.
6. Achieving the outcome.
7. Maintaining the outcome.
8. The next problem created by success.

For every core customer action, list obstacles through four lenses:

| Lens | Obstacle prompts |
|---|---|
| Outcome | Why might this not feel financially, emotionally, or strategically worthwhile? |
| Likelihood | Why might the buyer believe it will not work for someone like them? |
| Effort | What feels hard, confusing, unpleasant, risky, or disruptive? |
| Time | What takes too long, competes with priorities, or delays proof? |

Include practical, emotional, identity, switching, trust, and stakeholder obstacles. More complete problem coverage creates more opportunities for value.

## 4. Convert problems into solutions

Turn each obstacle into a positive solution requirement.

| Problem | Solution requirement |
|---|---|
| "I will not know what to do first." | Provide a guided first-step plan with clear ownership. |
| "Switching will interrupt operations." | Provide a transition process with continuity safeguards. |
| "My situation may be different." | Provide qualification, diagnosis, and adaptation rules. |

Use:

`How to help [buyer] achieve or complete [step] with less [effort/time/risk/cost] despite [specific obstacle].`

Do not promise a solution the business cannot deliver.

## 5. Generate delivery vehicles

Create several delivery options for each important solution using these dimensions.

### Attention level

- One-to-one.
- Small group.
- One-to-many.

### Customer effort

- DIY: customer implements from guidance or tools.
- Done with you: business guides implementation.
- Done for you: business performs the work.

### Medium

- In person.
- Phone or video.
- Email.
- Text, chat, or voice message.
- Written guide or checklist.
- Audio or video.
- Calculator, template, workflow, software, automation, or pre-built configuration.

### Service level

- Response time.
- Days and hours of availability.
- Frequency of contact.
- Proactive versus reactive support.
- Standardized versus customized delivery.
- Individual versus community accountability.

### Delivery matrix

For every serious option, record:

| Solution | Vehicle | Buyer value | Business cost | Scalability | Confidence | Required for promise? |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|

Use High/Medium/Low or evidence-based 1-5 scores. Avoid false precision.

## 6. Trim the list

Remove in this order:

1. High business cost and low buyer value.
2. Low business cost and low buyer value.

Retain:

- Low cost and high value whenever it supports the promise.
- High cost and high value only when price, capacity, or tiering makes it sustainable.

When a high-value option is too expensive, step back along the delivery dimensions until a scalable alternative preserves most of the value. Prefer reusable one-to-many assets when they genuinely reduce effort or increase certainty.

Do not remove a component required for the primary result simply to improve margin.

## 7. Stack the final package

For each retained component, connect:

`Problem → solution requirement → component name → delivery vehicle → buyer benefit → proof → delivery cost`

The package should:

- Solve the important perceived obstacles.
- Feel distinct from ordinary category alternatives.
- Be coherent rather than a pile of unrelated extras.
- Make the primary result easier, faster, and more believable.
- Be explainable in a logical sequence.

Classify every component:

- **Core:** required to deliver the primary promise.
- **Support:** improves adoption, speed, or certainty.
- **Bonus:** a distinct useful component highlighted in presentation. Prefer nonessential additions for time-limited bonuses. If a highlighted component is essential to the primary promise, keep it included and record that dependency; the book also highlights existing bundle components as bonuses.
- **Upsell or tier:** high-cost or high-access value better sold separately.

## 8. Balance sales and fulfillment

Recognize the continuum:

- More work and risk assumed by the business usually makes the offer easier to sell but harder to fulfill.
- Less work assumed by the business makes fulfillment easier but can weaken perceived value.

For an early offer, over-deliver enough to validate demand and learn the real obstacles. Then systematize, automate, or convert repeated work into reusable assets without reducing customer outcomes.

Do not optimize a delivery model that has not produced paid demand. Do not scale an offer that cannot be fulfilled.

## Core-offer deliverable

Provide:

1. Dream outcome.
2. Customer-journey problem map.
3. Problem-solution map.
4. Delivery-vehicle matrix.
5. Trim decisions with reasons.
6. Final stack classified as core, support, bonus, or tier.
7. Capacity and fulfillment risks.
8. The single recommended package.
