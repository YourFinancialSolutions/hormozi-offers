# Book Example Library

These are explanatory paraphrases of selected examples in the supplied PDF. They are not verbatim excerpts, independently verified case studies, or promises that another business will obtain the same outcomes. All page numbers are one-based PDF pages; see [book-map.md](book-map.md).

Use an example to transfer a mechanism, then return to the user's facts. Do not treat an author narrative, hypothetical illustration and measured customer outcome as interchangeable evidence.

## E1. Agency offer and cash comparison

**Type:** The author describes a real-business illustration with rounded numbers. **Source:** pp. 40–43; tables on pp. 40/42 were inspected visually.

**Starting point:** A conventional agency sells an initial $1,000 payment and a $1,000 monthly retainer. The proposed arrangement combines different payment/performance terms, lead handling, a result-related remedy and support for the client's sales process. The example changes several parts of the exchange, not only the headline.

| Reported table item | Commodity | Grand Slam |
|---|---:|---:|
| Advertising spend | $10,000 | $10,000 |
| Impressions | 300,000 | 300,000 |
| Appointments booked | 40 | 100 |
| Show rate | 75% | 75% |
| Appointments shown | 30 | 75 |
| Rounded close rate | 16% | 37% |
| Rounded purchases | 5 | 28 |
| Upfront price | $1,000 | $3,997 |
| Reported upfront total | $5,000 | $112,000 |

**Mechanism:** An appealing exchange increases response, a more convincing package increases closing, and different economics support more upfront cash. These effects compound.

**Correction:** The prose multiplication `2.5 × 2.5 × 4` equals 25, not 22.4. The table's reported totals give 22.4. Its close-rate ratio is approximately 2.31, and 28 × $3,997 is $111,916. The rows use rounding and should not be treated as an exact forecasting model. Cash collected before delivery/refunds is not profit.

**Transfer:** Compare old/new exposure, qualified response, attendance, close rate, collection amount, collection timing and costs. Do not borrow any of the example's multipliers as expected lift. Follow [market knowledge](knowledge-market-pricing.md).

## E2. Newspapers and resume help

**Type:** Author narratives. **Source:** pp. 46–50.

**Starting point:** A software business provides newspapers with an additional advertising-revenue opportunity and takes a share of what it creates. Despite attractive terms and capable selling, it struggles in a shrinking market. A separate resume-help example has people with acute needs who cannot afford the service because they are unemployed.

**Mechanism:** Neither a low-risk offer nor painful need is sufficient alone. The first example isolates market direction; the second isolates purchasing power. The newspaper entrepreneur's later move into mask production illustrates the author's point that the same operator can face very different demand conditions.

**Transfer question:** Which specific indicator is weak, and can changing the buyer/payer solve it? Do not infer that all newspapers are currently unviable, or that all unemployed people cannot buy services. The stories are contextual illustrations, not current market research.

## E3. Time-management specificity ladder

**Type:** Pricing illustration attributed by the author to Dan Kennedy. **Source:** pp. 54–55.

The sequence moves from a general time-management course (illustrated around $19) to sales professionals ($99), outbound B2B sales representatives ($499), and a more specific power-tools/gardening sales subgroup (suggested $1,000–$2,000).

**Mechanism:** The specialized version can speak directly to the buyer's work, financial context and application. The core principles may remain similar, but relevance and usefulness become easier to see. This is not evidence that changing only a title produces a 100-fold price increase.

**Transfer:** Specialize examples, templates, implementation and proof with the avatar. Confirm reachable market size and willingness to pay before adopting the illustrated price. See [market knowledge](knowledge-market-pricing.md).

## E4. Gym Launch changes its delivery vehicle

**Type:** Author account and self-reported results. **Source:** pp. 66–68, 105–106.

The early model required travel and extensive on-site help. The author reports that a remote engagement, in which the gym owner performed the work with guidance, demonstrated that the process could transfer. The business could then serve more gyms without scaling travel at the same rate.

**Mechanism:** Operational experience identified what could be taught and systematized. A broadly similar destination could be delivered through a different division of work. Reduced provider effort did not automatically require reducing buyer value.

**Evidence limit:** The later financial results include a voluntary event survey of 158 gyms after roughly 11 months in the program. These are the author's reported observations, not a controlled estimate or a forecast for new clients.

**Transfer:** Identify which buyer capabilities make DWY feasible before converting a DFY offer. A remote model fails if the required work exceeds the customer's resources. See [construction knowledge](knowledge-construction.md).

## E5. Transit arrival display

**Type:** Explanatory anecdote reported by the author, with related examples inspired by Rory Sutherland. **Source:** pp. 76–77.

The book contrasts expensive train-speed improvements with a display showing when the next train will arrive. The display makes waiting more predictable and less uncomfortable without changing the underlying journey in the same way.

**Mechanism:** Value can improve by removing uncertainty and improving an experience, not only through expensive engineering. The source's cost and satisfaction comparisons are not independently checked here.

**Transfer:** Show an honest status, expected next event, owner and recovery path in a service with unavoidable waits. Do not animate fake progress or claim the underlying work is faster when only visibility changed. See [value knowledge](knowledge-value.md).

## E6. Experienced surgeon

**Type:** Thought experiment. **Source:** pp. 79–80.

The buyer compares being an early patient with being a patient of a highly experienced surgeon. The nominal service and duration can be similar, while confidence differs greatly.

**Mechanism:** Perceived likelihood affects willingness to pay. The buyer wants to believe the result can occur for them; quantity of experience is one signal, not proof of any individual outcome.

**Transfer:** Use relevant experience, inspectable process and comparable results. If a service has no track record, demonstrate a bounded pilot instead of inventing testimonials. This example explains marketing perception, not how to choose medical treatment.

## E7. Early wins and paid speed

**Type:** Author operating example plus category comparisons. **Source:** pp. 81–82.

The gym-business discussion distinguishes a large long-term revenue ambition from early campaign activation and an initial sale. A useful early experience reinforces the decision and helps a customer continue. The same passage discusses paying for faster delivery or service where slower/free alternatives exist.

**Mechanism:** The final result motivates purchase; meaningful intermediate progress can support retention and implementation. Speed is valuable when the wait matters to the buyer.

**Transfer:** Define a first usable output that predicts progress and is within the provider's control. Do not copy the example's dollar amount or seven-day sales goal as a universal promise. See [value knowledge](knowledge-value.md).

## E8. Brick exercise and staffing arithmetic

**Type:** Contrasting exercises. **Source:** pp. 89–93.

The fixed staffing example calculates sales per representative and the number of representatives needed. The brick exercise asks for many uses, then changes assumed size, material and shape to reveal further options.

**Mechanism:** One task converges on a constrained answer; the other expands possible solutions. Offer work needs exploration before cost and capacity narrow the choices.

**Transfer:** Change attention, medium, customer effort or response-time assumptions in a service before selecting a vehicle. A 120-second ideation exercise is optional; repeat it only when it opens useful alternatives. See [construction knowledge](knowledge-construction.md).

## E9. Fitness customer journey

**Type:** Author construction example. **Source:** pp. 95–100.

The starting offer is compared as a gym membership. The author shifts focus to a desired physical result and maps shopping, cooking, eating and exercising, then obstacles involving time, expense, confusion, family and travel.

**Mechanism:** The unit of design becomes the complete path to a result, including perceived reasons for failure. Turning each obstacle into a solution requirement precedes deciding how to fulfill it.

**Transfer:** Map the user's real journey before listing package components. The book's weight-loss target is historical source wording, not a recommended health claim. A complete obstacle map does not mean unlimited scope for every possible buyer.

## E10. Relationship-program handwritten notes

**Type:** Working notes shared by Brooke Castillo, as described by the author. **Source:** pp. 100–102; handwritten images visually inspected.

The notes expand a relationship aspiration into difficulties involving partner availability, communication, time, insecurity, compatibility and expectations, then reframe those as solution goals. They are rough brainstorming pages rather than a polished final offer.

**Mechanism:** The problem-to-solution process transfers beyond fitness and includes emotional/social barriers. Technical neatness is less important in discovery than surfacing meaningful obstacles.

**Transfer:** Invite rough customer-language notes first, then organize them. Do not infer that every listed barrier can be eliminated or that the 90-day program concept guarantees a relationship.

## E11. Executive who eats out

**Type:** Author sales anecdote. **Source:** pp. 110–111.

The author's original fitness delivery required home-prepared meals. A prospective client regularly ate lunch out. Instead of insisting on a change she would not make, he offered to create a restaurant-eating guide. It addressed the objection and became an asset for future buyers.

**Mechanism:** A single apparently minor incompatibility can stop a purchase. Redesigning around a real-life constraint can improve both the offer and future fulfillment efficiency.

**Transfer:** When a buyer says “that won't fit my situation,” determine whether an alternate vehicle can solve the obstacle. Do not assume every request is feasible or accept unlimited customization; evaluate whether the solution repeats. See [construction knowledge](knowledge-construction.md).

## E12. Meal-planning spreadsheet and final stack

**Type:** Author operating example. **Source:** pp. 112–116.

The author reports spending about 100 hours building a spreadsheet that generated personalized meals, shopping needs and preparation instructions, reducing later plan creation to about 15 minutes. The final package groups help for shopping, cooking, eating, exercise, travel, adherence and eating out.

**Mechanism:** High fixed creation effort can support low marginal fulfillment cost. Grouping by customer problem makes many assets understandable as a coherent system.

**Transfer:** Compare creation cost, actual reuse, support and maintenance; do not call software costless. The book's package value anchors and sale price illustrate presentation, not independently verified market prices for each component. Avoid double-counting shared orientation work.

## E13. Workshop supply scenarios

**Type:** Hypothetical willingness-to-pay illustration. **Source:** pp. 125–128.

Ten buyers at $500 generate $5,000, while two private workshops at $5,000 each generate $10,000. The discussion emphasizes premium access and leaving some demand unfulfilled for future availability.

**Mechanism:** A smaller group paying more can be commercially preferable when the value of access and willingness to pay support it. The higher-price version changes the experience too; it is not a controlled price-only experiment.

**Transfer:** Compare delivery hours, contribution and future demand under both structures. Do not assume a fixed fraction of buyers will pay a fixed multiple or that turning people away always improves profit. See [demand knowledge](knowledge-demand.md).

## E14. Distinct components as bonuses and partner benefits

**Type:** Presentation guidance and illustrative arrangements. **Source:** pp. 147–153.

The author explains highlighting useful parts of an existing bundle, connecting each to an obstacle, and increasing visible value after establishing the core price. Adjacent partners can contribute benefits in exchange for qualified exposure; some arrangements also include commissions.

**Mechanism:** The customer notices concrete help they might otherwise overlook. A reusable tool removes effort; a genuine negotiated benefit may add value at modest cost to the seller.

**Transfer:** Identify the obstacle, show use and evidence, substantiate the value, and keep essential support included. Do not copy the healthcare referral payments as legally approved arrangements. See [bonus knowledge](knowledge-bonuses.md).

## E15. Refund-count comparison

**Type:** Numerical illustration. **Source:** p. 156.

100 purchases with five refunds leave 95 retained purchases. 130 purchases with thirteen refunds leave 117. A stronger guarantee can therefore yield more retained customers despite a higher refund percentage.

**Mechanism:** Evaluate the absolute business outcome, not fear of an increased claim percentage alone.

**Correction:** The 23% increase concerns retained purchases. It is not automatically the same increase in money or bottom-line profit, especially when refunded clients consume delivery work. Compare costs, cash timing and capacity using [guarantee knowledge](knowledge-guarantees.md). The change in conversion and claims is assumed, not predicted.

## E16. Float session and MAGIC

**Type:** Naming illustration. **Source:** pp. 171–178; MAGIC diagram visually inspected on p. 173.

The same underlying float-center service is described through a functional session label or a time-bounded stress-relief program. MAGIC then explains magnet, avatar, goal, interval and container, with flexible ordering and usually three to five components.

**Mechanism:** The wrapper helps the right person recognize purpose and format. Refreshing it can renew attention while a proven delivery process remains stable.

**Transfer:** Keep the real inclusions and terms constant in a naming test. Changing an actual service duration is an operational change; changing equivalent time units is not. Do not assume the source's example headlines comply with current advertising rules. See [naming knowledge](knowledge-naming-execution.md).
